It was around noon on an ordinary Wednesday in autumn when the payment gateways for Wallex and other cryptocurrency exchanges shut down without warning.

In a moment, the familiar route from signing up to funding an account was gone. Our conversion from signup to first deposit fell by approximately 89%.

We had to help people move forward, while answering a much harder question: how do you ask someone to trust a new way of moving money when the familiar one has just disappeared?

My responsibility grew with the problem.

I initially contributed as a design lead, with hands-on design support to reopen Wallex deposits. For the redesign, I became both product owner and designer, using Shape Up and coordinating the work through release.

01 · The immediate response

Be clear about what is unavailable.

The first decision was about honesty and speed. We made the unavailable state visible and offered WallGold, Wallex’s gold-investing platform, as an alternative for users whose immediate goal was investment.

This had a clear limit: buying gold did not fulfill the intent of someone trying to buy crypto. It was a temporary option while we worked to restore deposits into Wallex.

02 · Restore access

Three days to design. One week to ship.

By Saturday, there was still no clear end to the shutdown. Waiting was no longer a useful product strategy. We set the previous first-deposit conversion as our recovery benchmark and worked with our payment partners on another route.

Sender information from card-to-card and reference-based transfers could be matched to the information we collected from users. That gave us a way to reconcile transfers and credit the corresponding Wallex accounts.

The constraint

Roughly three days for design and three more for development.

The priority

Restore a usable deposit route, then learn from the actual journey.

The release restored access by the end of the week. Average activation recovered to approximately half of its previous level. That was meaningful progress, but also a clear sign that availability alone was not enough.

03 · Read the behavior

The route people chose was not the route they needed.

We looked at navigation paths alongside completed deposits. People repeatedly entered the “Direct Payment” activation flow, apparently expecting the familiar gateway. Yet card-to-card transfers accounted for roughly three quarters of completed deposits.

The mismatch made the naming problem concrete. We changed the label from “Direct Payment” to “Automatic Payment.”

04 · Rebuild the experience

Make the next step easier to understand.

With deposits available again, I took ownership of the next phase as both product owner and designer. Working with Shape Up, I coordinated the redesign through release.

The work centered on three questions: which method should I use, how do I transfer correctly, and when will the money arrive?

Start with the amount, not a list of methods.

Each payment method had a different limit. Showing those limits on the cards still left people contacting support to work out which one to use. I redesigned the choice around the amount users intended to deposit.

Entering an amount made eligibility explicit: unsupported methods remained visible but disabled. We aimed to recommend the fastest eligible route, updating the recommendation as the amount changed. The top of the page also brought rotating activity messages and support into view, providing reassurance before an unfamiliar financial task.

Evidence module preview

Confirm the source before the user leaves.

Missing source-card details and transfers from someone else’s card created preventable errors. Immediately after method selection, we asked people to choose a saved card or add their own.

The transfer instructions then made the movement of money explicit: from this registered source, to this Wallex destination. Guidance and the post-transfer action stayed in the same journey.

Evidence module preview

Teach the banking step where it happens.

“Transfer to this card” was not enough. Every bank had its own labels, navigation, and sequence. Covering every banking app at once would have expanded the scope considerably.

We prioritized the five banks responsible for more than 80% of incoming transfers and placed step-by-step guidance inside the flow. That concentrated the effort on the journeys most people were taking.

Evidence module preview

Design for the wait after the transfer.

Sending the money did not end the user’s journey. Until their Wallex balance updated, many contacted support to ask when the funds would arrive.

We added “I’ve completed the transfer.” Users entered the method, source, and date and time. The next screen gave an expected arrival time based on those details, rather than leaving them to guess.

Evidence module preview

If the expected window had passed, the experience changed with it. We acknowledged the longer wait, offered a direct route to support, and explained common causes with the relevant next action.

05 · The outcome

Recovery beyond the previous baseline.

After the final release, average conversion from signup to first deposit reached approximately 9% above its pre-disruption baseline, and approximately 119% above the first transfer-based release.

During the first few days after launch, conversion briefly peaked around 38% above the old baseline. That early peak is shown separately from the final average.

What I took from the project

The journey ends when the money is usable.

The shutdown began as an infrastructure problem. Recovering from it required coordinated decisions about payment operations, product scope, interaction design, and support.

Our first release gave people a way to deposit again. The redesign made it easier to choose, act, and understand what would happen next. Owning both product and design helped me connect those decisions to a single recovery objective.

A deposit experience needs to support the user all the way to having their money available to use.
